# Walking Andrew through Educa8

For Aidan. Monday. Roughly forty minutes if he has questions, twenty if he does not.

This is the plain-English version. Andrew never needs to hear a field name, a table count or
the word "backend", and none of that appears below. The build cost lives in
`docs/PLUMBING.md` and belongs in the Jawad conversation, not this one.

**One link opens everything:** `https://aidanjsogorka.github.io/origna8-demos/educa8/`

There is a bar across the bottom of every screen. Use it to move around rather than going back
to the hub each time.

---

## What you actually need out of this meeting

Two answers, and only two. Everything else is showing.

1. **Is the writing job realistic?** Two of the three learner versions need Andrew to write
   something he has never had to write before, and only he can say whether that is a week of
   work or a month.
2. **Which version does he think loan officers will actually open?** He talks to them. You do
   not.

Everything below is in service of getting those two answers. If you get them in the first ten
minutes, stop showing and start talking.

---

## Before you open anything

**If he is short on time, show him one thing.** There is a side by side table at the top of the
hub comparing A, B and C across eleven plain rows: points, ranking, streak, what decides the
reading order, who else can see how you are doing, and what Andrew has to write. It says more in
thirty seconds than this walkthrough does in twenty minutes. Show that, then jump to the two
questions at the end.

If you have the full forty minutes, say this first, because it changes how he reads the rest:

> "Educa8 today is six cards that all say Not Started. Nobody opens it. We rebuilt it twice,
> because there are two honest answers to why anybody would, and I want you to tell me which
> one is right. Nothing here is final and none of it is built yet."

Then set his expectation about his own role:

> "The thing I need from you is not a design opinion. It is whether the writing job on the back
> of this is something you can actually do."

---

## Stop 1. His screen, the course studio

**Open:** Course studio, from the bottom bar.

This is the one screen that is unambiguously his, so start here. It builds goodwill before you
ask him for anything.

What to point at:

- **It is a writing tool, not a form.** He opens a course, writes a lesson, sets the question at
  the end, and presses publish. That is the whole loop.
- **It is open on a half-finished course on purpose.** Rate Lock Strategies, three lessons
  written and the fourth empty, because that is what his Tuesday actually looks like.
- **"How long it takes" is measured, not guessed.** Type into the lesson and the word count and
  the minute estimate move. He never has to pick a number.
- **Preview as a learner.** The single most useful button on the screen. He can see exactly what
  an LO sees before anybody else does.

**Then show the switch, and be careful how you frame it.** Top right, "Show the plumbing".

> "This one is not for you. It is the same screen with every label swapped for what the
> developer calls it. It exists so Jawad and I never have to make you learn his words. Flip it
> off and forget it exists."

That framing matters. If he thinks the technical view is something he is expected to understand,
he will decide the whole tool is above him.

**Ask:** "Is there anything you do today, writing this stuff, that this screen would stop you
doing?"

---

## Stop 2. Version A, competition

**Open:** Competition, from the bottom bar. The screen is headed "Where you stand".

> "This is the version that says the reason you open a training section is to win something."

Point at three things, in this order:

1. **One thing highlighted.** Not six cards. Exactly one lesson to do next, with how long it
   takes and what it pays side by side, and everything else on the screen is quieter than it.
2. **The branch board.** Finishing a lesson moves your row in front of the other sixteen people
   on the branch, this week.
3. **Show it moving.** Scroll to the reading view at the bottom, answer the mentor's question,
   pick the escrow answer. The score counts up and the board reorders. Let him watch it.

Then say the honest thing, because he will think it anyway and you want to be the one who says
it first:

> "The risk here is obvious. It motivates the top three people on the branch and it puts the
> bottom half on a list, by name, every week. And the people who most need the training are the
> ones who will be at the bottom."

**Ask:** "Would the LOs you talk to find that fun, or would they find it humiliating?"

That question is the whole reason A exists. He will have an instinct and it is worth more than
your instinct.

---

## Stop 3. Version B, relevance

**Open:** Useful, from the bottom bar. The screen is headed "What your files need".

> "This is the version that says nobody opens training to win. They skip it because it feels
> disconnected from the work. So this one runs off your actual loans."

Four things, in this order:

1. **The top of the page is a real appointment.** Thursday, two o'clock, an FHA cash-out, and it
   is this officer's first FHA file. The fifteen minutes on offer is the fifteen minutes that
   makes Thursday go better. The screen says so out loud: "picked by this loan, not by a course".
2. **"Your loans."** Twelve of them open. Seven need something the officer has never read. Three of those have an appointment this week. That is the entire ordering rule and
   the page says so out loud.
3. **"What keeps going wrong, and how we know."** This is the part to slow down on. Every line
   points at something that already happened on their own files: a condition that came back three
   times for the same reason, two disclosures that went out late, a rate lock that was extended
   when it did not need to be. It is not a quiz result.

   Point out the section underneath it, "And what you are already good at", and say why it is there:

   > "That half is not decoration. Without it this screen is a performance review, and nobody
   > opens a performance review twice."

4. **"What you can run."** Nine things an LO actually does, and for each one: have you read it,
   did you still know it a week later, and have you done it on a real file. Three rows are orange:
   there is a live loan on the desk and nothing behind it. Say the line plainly:

   > "Those three rows are the argument. He is running an FHA cash-out today and has never read a
   > word about it."

**Then the cost, in his terms, and do not soften it:**

> "This version needs something from you that A does not. Somebody has to write a list of about
> forty things a loan officer needs to know, and then say which kind of loan needs which ones.
> FHA purchase needs the case number rule and the appraisal rule. Self-employed borrower needs
> the income documents. That list does not exist anywhere, and it is writing, so it is yours, not
> Jawad's."

**Ask, and this is the most important question in the meeting:** "Is that a week of work or is
that three months? Be pessimistic."

If he says three months, B is in real trouble and you need to know that on Monday, not in
September.

---

## Stop 4. Version C

**Open:** Useful, tidied, from the bottom bar.

Keep this short. It is a design note, not a decision he owns.

> "Same as B, same words, same numbers. I thought B was getting cluttered so I moved the most
> important panel to the top and turned one table into something you read at a glance. If this
> one feels calmer to you, it replaces B. If you cannot tell the difference, that is also a
> useful answer."

Show the one thing that changed shape: the row of small squares near the bottom right. Each
square is one thing they were taught, and how full it is shows how well they would still
remember it today. Amber means it has gone stale.

**Ask:** "Does C feel calmer than B, or the same?" Then move on. Do not spend ten minutes here.

---

## Stop 5. The five design directions, only if there is time

**Open:** the hub. There is a side by side table near the top comparing all three, which is the
fastest way to show him the differences. Then scroll to "How we got here".

> "Before any of this we built the same content five different ways to choose the look by
> comparison instead of by argument. We picked the fifth. The other four are still up because the
> reasons they lost are useful."

Thirty seconds. Do not walk him through all five. This is context, not a decision.

---

## The two questions again, at the end

Close by asking both directly, because he may not volunteer them:

1. **"About forty concepts, and which loan types need which. Realistic for you, or not?"**
2. **"Which one do you think they actually open on a Tuesday?"**

Write down what he says in his own words. If he hedges on the first one, push once: ask him to
guess how long the first ten would take him. Ten is enough to know.

---

## Things not to say

- **Do not say "gamification".** It sounds like a toy and it will make him defensive about
  content he takes seriously.
- **Do not mention tables, events, or scheduled jobs.** Wrong meeting. If he asks how hard it is
  to build, say "A is the expensive one, B is cheaper to build but needs the list from you," and
  leave it there.
- **Do not ask him to choose the visual direction.** That is settled and re-opening it costs a
  week.
- **Do not promise a date.** Nothing is built yet.

## Things worth admitting

- **Nothing here is connected to anything.** Every name and number is invented. Say so early or
  he will assume the pipeline data is real and get alarmed about whose loans those are.
- **These are laptop-sized only.** If he opens it on a phone it will look wrong. Have him look on
  the laptop.
- **The demo is dark only.** The real app has a light mode and these do not.

---

## If he pushes back on the whole idea

The likeliest objection is "the content is fine, people just do not open it." That is agreement,
not an objection. The answer is:

> "Agreed, and that is what this is. We did not touch your lessons. All three versions use your
> six courses and your twenty eight guides, word for word. The only thing that changed is what
> the screen does to make somebody start one."

The second likeliest is "loan officers will not do a quiz." Worth taking seriously, and the
answer is that it is one question, it takes about twenty seconds, and it comes back three days
later because that is when people forget. In B and C it is asked against one of their own live
files, which is the part that makes it feel like work rather than school.
