Mortgage Fundamentals · Chapter one of five

What Is a Mortgage?

Four words carry most of this course: principal, interest, amortization, escrow. Everything after chapter one is a variation on how they interact.

15 min read 4 definitions in the margin 1 figure · 1 table Check not yet taken
Section one

The collateral is the whole idea

A mortgage is a loan used to purchase real estate, where the property itself serves as collateral. That last clause is the part borrowers skip and the part that explains everything else you will learn here.

Because the house secures the debt, the lender's exposure is tied to something it can appraise, inspect, insure and, in the worst case, take back. Almost every rule in this course exists to protect that link. Title work protects it. Hazard insurance protects it. Appraisal review, reserve requirements and occupancy certification all protect it.

An unsecured loan prices the borrower alone. A mortgage prices the borrower and the property together. That is why a file with a 780 credit score and eleven months of reserves can still stall on a survey line or an appraisal that lands under contract price.1

Collateral n.

Property pledged against a debt. If the loan fails, the lender can take the property instead of the money.

Section two

Four words, and the arithmetic between them

The key concepts are defined in the margin, not in the sentence, because you will look them up later and prose is a poor place to store a definition. What matters in the running text is how they behave together.

Principal shrinks. Interest is charged on whatever principal is left, so it shrinks with it, slowly. Amortization is the schedule that holds the total payment flat while the mix inside it moves. Escrow sits outside all three: it rides along on the same monthly draft but never touches the loan balance.2

A borrower will almost never ask you what amortization means. They will ask why twelve months of payments moved their balance by about four thousand four hundred dollars. Those are the same question, and only one of them is answerable with a definition.

Principal n.

The amount borrowed.

Interest n.

The cost of borrowing.

Amortization n.

How the loan is paid off over time.

Escrow n.

Funds held for taxes and insurance.

Section three

Where the first payment actually goes

Take a 400,000 dollar loan at 6.5 percent over 30 years. The payment is fixed at 2,528.34 for principal and interest. The split inside that number is not fixed at all, and month one is the least flattering month of the entire schedule.

Interest is calculated on the balance at the start of the period, and in month one the balance is the largest it will ever be. Nothing is being hidden. The arithmetic is simply front-loaded by design, and it stays that way for years.

Rounding

Figures assume no extra payments and a fixed rate held for the full term. Live pricing sits in the Pricing tab.

Worth memorising

Balance times rate, divided by twelve, gives month one interest. 400,000 × .065 ÷ 12 = 2,166.67. You can do this on a call.

Section four

Why any of this matters at your desk

A borrower who understands the split at application does not call you in month nine to complain about it. The conversation costs ninety seconds up front and saves a defensive one later, usually with someone who has already decided you were not straight with them.

This is the pattern for the rest of the course. Each mechanism has a borrower-facing sentence and a file-facing consequence, and the LOs who last learn both at the same time.

In the margin · Mentor
You have the arithmetic. Now say it out loud: a borrower asks why their balance barely moved in a year. Thirty seconds, no jargon. What do you actually say?

"Your payment is the same every month, but early on most of it is interest, because interest is charged on what you still owe and right now that is almost the whole loan. It flips over time. Around year nineteen more of your payment goes to the balance than to interest, and anything extra you send goes straight at the balance."

No numbers unless they ask. If they ask, use Table 1.

Retrieval check · two questions · unattempted

Close the chapter

Question one
A borrower on a 30-year fixed asks why twelve months of payments cut the balance by only about 4,400 dollars. What is the accurate answer?

b. Fees are settled at closing and are not inside the monthly payment. Escrow is collected in addition to principal and interest, never out of it. The balance moves slowly because interest is priced off a balance that has barely moved.

Question two
A file has strong credit and eleven months of reserves, but the appraisal comes in 20,000 dollars under contract. Why does that threaten the loan?

c. The collateral, not the contract, sets the ceiling. Loan-to-value is calculated on the lower of the two, so the gap has to be closed with cash, a renegotiation, or a challenge to the appraisal.
